# Limitations and Future Work

While the proposed two-stage physics-informed deep learning pipeline—combining a self-supervised Physics-Informed Autoencoder (PIA) with a spatial U-Net Refiner—demonstrates substantial performance gains, including a 6,431× computational speedup over classical non-linear least squares (NLLS) fitting and a 14.5-fold reduction in estimation error at SNR = 10, several methodological and practical limitations must be acknowledged.

First, the evaluation was conducted exclusively on synthetic datasets derived from 1000 digital breast phantoms generated by the Virtual Imaging Clinical Trials for Regulatory Evaluation (VICTRE) platform. Although the VICTRE framework models realistic Rician noise derived from k-space sampling and provides ground-truth voxel-wise parameter maps, it does not encapsulate the full spectrum of physical artifacts encountered in clinical breast diffusion-weighted MRI (DW-MRI). In particular, the simulation platform does not model bulk patient motion (e.g., respiratory or cardiac movement), $B_1$ transmit field inhomogeneities, fat suppression failures, eddy-current-induced geometric distortions, magnetic susceptibility artifacts near tissue-air boundaries, or inter-scanner variability across manufacturers and field strengths (1.5T vs. 3.0T).

Second, the model is trained and evaluated using a fixed acquisition protocol consisting of eight specific $b$-values ($b = [0, 5, 50, 100, 200, 500, 800, 1000]\text{ s/mm}^2$). Clinical breast DW-MRI protocols vary considerably across institutions in both the number and selection of $b$-values. Because the input layer of the Stage 1 autoencoder expects an exact 8-channel diffusion decay signal, deploying the model on clinical data with alternative $b$-value schedules would require network retraining, architectural adaptation (e.g., $b$-value encoding networks), or signal interpolation.

Third, the current architecture operates strictly in 2D on individual spatial slices of matrix size $200 \times 200$ pixels. By evaluating slices independently, the model does not exploit 3D volumetric spatial context along the slice-encoding direction, which could provide additional regularization for complex volumetric lesions. Furthermore, clinical breast MRI scans vary in matrix size and spatial resolution; because the receptive fields of the Stage 2 U-Net Refiner were trained on fixed $200 \times 200$ grids, applying the pipeline to acquisitions with different spatial dimensions or anisotropic slice thickness may alter refiner behavior or require spatial resampling.

Fourth, the network yields deterministic point estimates for the IVIM parameters ($f$, $D_t$, and $D^*$) without accompanying uncertainty quantification. In clinical practice, voxel-wise confidence intervals or predictive variance metrics (e.g., Bayesian neural networks or Monte Carlo dropout) are desirable to inform radiologists when parameter estimates may be unreliable due to extreme noise or out-of-distribution signal profiles.

To address these limitations, future research will focus on several key clinical translation avenues. We plan to explore transfer learning and domain adaptation strategies to fine-tune the phantom-pretrained networks on clinical breast DW-MRI scans without requiring dense voxel-wise ground truth. In parallel, we intend to extend the architecture to accommodate variable $b$-value inputs and 3D volumetric images, incorporate physics-guided uncertainty quantification, and conduct multi-center prospective reader studies to evaluate the diagnostic utility of the reconstructed IVIM maps in differentiating benign from malignant breast lesions.
